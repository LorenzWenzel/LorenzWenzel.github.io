Every RAG system starts with a decision you usually don't make but inherit: what
is actually being searched? The answer has three levels — full text, vectors,
both — and on rental contracts they behave more differently than the literature
suggests.

This post is only about finding. What happens to the hit list afterwards is
covered in a post of its own.

## BM25: counting, but properly

BM25 is the standard formula for full-text search and has been hard-wired into
Azure AI Search since July 2020 — older services could still use
`ClassicSimilarity`, new ones accept nothing else
([docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/index-similarity-and-scoring)).

The idea fits in one sentence: **a hit counts for more the more often the term
appears in the document, the rarer it is across the whole corpus, and the shorter
the document.** Three factors, and all three matter for contracts.

### Rare beats frequent

The second factor — inverse document frequency — is the most important. I
recomputed it on a miniature corpus of six contract sections:

| Term | in how many sections | IDF |
|---|---:|---:|
| `der` | 6 of 6 | 0.074 |
| `kaution` | 3 of 6 | 0.693 |
| `mietvertrag` | 2 of 6 | 1.030 |
| `gut57` | 1 of 6 | **1.540** |
| `03.12` | 1 of 6 | **1.540** |

A property code occurring exactly once weighs twenty times an article. Ask for
`GUT57 WE 03.12` and of six sections **one** remains with a score of 4.30 — all
others at zero. That isn't a ranking any more, that's a hit.

### What k1 does

The first factor is subtler. You might assume a term appearing twenty times is
twenty times as relevant. BM25 explicitly does not assume that:

![Vier Kurven für k1 = 0; 0,5; 1,2 und 3,0. Bei k1 = 0 bleibt der Beitrag konstant bei 1×, unabhängig davon wie oft der Begriff vorkommt. Bei größerem k1 wächst der Beitrag mit der Häufigkeit, flacht aber ab — bei k1 = 1,2 erreicht er nach zwanzig Vorkommen etwa das Doppelte.](/assets/img/bm25-saettigung.svg)

At the Azure default `k1 = 1.2`, the twentieth occurrence of a term is worth just
2.08 times the first. The curve saturates. The documentation gives the reason: on
a search for "Apollo Spaceflight," an article about Greek mythology containing
"Apollo" dozens of times and "Spaceflight" never should not beat an article that
mentions both words a few times each.

For contracts that means: a paragraph writing "Kaution" eight times does **not**
beat one that defines it once by a factor of eight. Rightly so — the definition
is usually in the paragraph that mentions the word once.

### What b does

The third factor normalizes for document length, default `b = 0.75`. At `b = 0`
length is irrelevant, at `b = 1` it's fully normalized. Concretely, for the same
term with the same frequency in a half-length versus a double-length chunk:

| b | short chunk | long chunk | ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.00 | 1.375 | 1.375 | 1.00 |
| 0.50 | 1.517 | 1.158 | 1.31 |
| **0.75** | **1.600** | **1.073** | **1.49** |
| 1.00 | 1.692 | 1.000 | 1.69 |

That's a quiet connection to chunking: produce chunks of widely varying length —
and cutting along the paragraphs does exactly that — and you systematically
penalize the long ones. With a fixed token target as in the POC, `b` hardly
matters, because all chunks are roughly the same length.

### Where BM25 stops

At language. None of the contracts contains the word "Kündigungsfrist" when the
paragraph is titled "Beendigung des Mietverhältnisses." BM25 searches character
strings, not meanings. The German analyzer `de.microsoft`, which the POC applies
to `heading` and `content`, catches part of this — it lemmatizes and decomposes
compounds, so it finds "Mietverhältnis" inside "Mietverhältnisses"
([docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/index-add-language-analyzers)).
But it doesn't turn a paraphrase into a term.

## Embeddings: meaning, but blurry

Vector search inverts the problem. Text is mapped to a point in a
high-dimensional space — in the POC via `text-embedding-3-small` with 1,536
dimensions — and proximity in that space is meant to be similarity in meaning.
"Wann kann ich raus aus dem Vertrag?" then lands near "Beendigung des
Mietverhältnisses" without sharing a single word.

### From words to vectors

Where that proximity comes from is worth a short detour, because it explains why
embeddings fail on identifiers of all things.

The basic assumption is as old as it is plain: words appearing in similar
contexts have similar meanings — *"a word is characterized by the company it
keeps."* To exploit it you look, across millions of texts, at which words sit to
the left and right of a target word.

An example with a window of two neighboring words:

- "Der **König** regiert das Land."
- "Der **Herrscher** regiert das Land."
- "Die **Königin** regiert das Land."

"König," "Herrscher" and "Königin" appear in almost identical contexts —
surrounded by "Der/Die" and "regiert das Land." A model trained to predict the
target word from the context (or vice versa) learns from this that the three
words are semantically alike, and assigns them correspondingly nearby vectors.
"Fahrrad" or "Banane" appear in entirely different contexts and land far away in
the vector space.

That core idea is unchanged to this day. What has changed is the architecture
implementing it. Instead of a fixed two-word window and a shallow neural network
— as in word2vec — today's models with deep transformer architectures and
self-attention consider the entire sentence or paragraph at once and dynamically
weight which words are relevant to the meaning of another word. This makes
embeddings **context-dependent** — "Bank" gets a different vector depending on
the sentence ("Ich sitze auf der Bank" vs. "Ich gehe zur Bank"), which the simple
neighborhood principle alone could not achieve. Only the compute and the training
data volumes available today make these considerably more complex models
practical.

For contracts that's no side note: the same principle that pulls "König" and
"Herrscher" together also pulls `WE 03.12` and `WE 03.13` together — both appear
in practically identical contexts, and neither helps the model tell them apart.
What is a strength for meaning is a weakness for identity.

Azure AI Search searches this space with **HNSW**, an approximation method: a
hierarchical graph in which every point is connected to up to `m` neighbors and
the search works its way from coarse to fine layers. `efConstruction` (default
400) controls how carefully the graph is built, `efSearch` how broadly it's
searched at runtime
([docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/vector-search-ranking)).
The method is *approximate* — it usually finds the nearest neighbors, not
guaranteed. If you need the guarantee, use `exhaustive: true` and pay in runtime.

One detail that readily leads to wrong conclusions: **the returned score is not
the cosine.** Azure computes `1 / (1 + cosine distance)`, landing in the range
0.333 to 1.00. Set a threshold on "cosine > 0.8" and you're setting it on
something other than you think; the docs supply the conversion back.

### Where embeddings stop

At identifiers. I ran what carries identity in these contracts through the
tokenizer:

```
GUT57      -> 3 Token: ['G', 'UT', '57']
SOD118     -> 3 Token: ['S', 'OD', '118']
WE 03.12   -> 5 Token: ['WE', ' ', '03', '.', '12']
ME#0.02    -> 5 Token: ['ME', '#', '0', '.', '02']
```

`GUT57` does not exist as a unit for the model. It breaks into three fragments,
none of which means the code — `UT` and `57` occur in a thousand other contexts.
The vector for `GUT57` therefore sits somewhere among the vectors of arbitrary
other letter-digit combinations, and `WE 03.12` and `WE 03.13` lie practically on
top of each other.

For prose that's the desired property: similar things should be close together.
For file numbers it's exactly wrong.

## Hybrid: not a compromise, but a complement

Each method fails at what the other is good at. That's why hybrid search is the
default today — not caution, but division of labor.

The obvious implementation would be to normalize both scores and add them. Azure
AI Search explicitly does **not** do that, and the numbers say why: BM25 scores
are unbounded above, vector scores lie between 0.333 and 1.00. The scales have
nothing to do with each other, and a conversion would have to be recalibrated per
corpus.

Instead, **reciprocal rank fusion** is used: the arithmetic runs on *ranks*, not
scores. Each list contributes `1 / (k + rank)` for each document, the
contributions are summed, and `k` is a constant — in Azure, fixed at 60
([docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/hybrid-search-ranking)).

![Drei Spalten: die BM25-Trefferliste, die Vektor-Trefferliste und die fusionierte Liste. Jeder Platz steuert 1 durch 60 plus Platz bei. Dokumente, die in beiden Listen vorkommen, landen oben; Dokumente aus nur einer Liste rutschen nach unten.](/assets/img/rrf-fusion.svg)

The advantage is precisely that **the scales don't matter**. It makes no
difference whether BM25 returns a score of 4.3 or 43 — only the rank counts. That
makes the method robust against corpus changes, against different embedding
models, and against the fact that BM25 scores vary by shard.

### The property this produces

More interesting than the robustness is what RRF implicitly rewards:

| Case | RRF score |
|---|---:|
| Rank 5 in **both** lists | 0.03077 |
| Rank 1 in **only one** list | 0.01639 |

**Agreement beats dominance by a factor of 1.9.** A document both methods find
moderately good lands ahead of one that only a single method considers perfect.
With two lists this is so pronounced that a document ranked 1 in one list and only
100 in the other still comes out ahead (0.02264 against 0.01639).

For contracts that's the right default. The question "Kündigungsfrist bei
SOD118?" has a lexical component (`SOD118`) and a semantic one (`Kündigungsfrist`
≈ `Beendigung`). The chunk you want is the one both methods find plausible — not
the one a single method finds perfect.

### Four numbers all called "k"

The most common confusion in hybrid queries, and the docs warn about it
explicitly:

![Vier Kästen nebeneinander: maxTextRecallSize mit Standard 1000, k_nearest_neighbors mit 60, die RRF-Konstante k fest 60 als gestrichelter Kasten, und top mit 60. Nur drei davon steuern Mengen.](/assets/img/hybrid-vier-zahlen.svg)

The RRF constant is **not a quantity**. Raising it fetches not one additional hit
— it only flattens how strongly the leading ranks are weighted against the
trailing ones. With small `k`, rank 1 dominates everything; with large `k`, all
ranks converge. The value 60 comes from the original work by Cormack, Clarke and
Büttcher (2009) and has stuck ever since.

The quantities are the other three. In the POC:

```python
vq = VectorizedQuery(vector=q_emb, k_nearest_neighbors=topK0, fields="embedding")

doc_client.search(
    search_text=query,        # BM25-Seite
    vector_queries=[vq],      # Vektorseite
    top=topK0,
)
```

Two queries run in parallel, RRF merges them, `top` determines what comes out.
`maxTextRecallSize` isn't set and therefore stands at 1,000 — the full-text side
searches considerably more broadly than the 60 suggests, and feeds RRF a
correspondingly long list.

## What I take from this

**Hybrid here isn't the cautious middle ground, it's the only variant that can
serve both question types.** With a corpus of prose you could argue for pure
vector search; with one where `GUT57` and `WE 03.12` carry the identity, you
can't.

---

**In short:** BM25 weights rare terms highly, saturates on frequent repetition
(`k1`) and normalizes for document length (`b`) — exactly right for property
codes, blind to paraphrases. Embeddings handle paraphrases but break `GUT57` into
`['G', 'UT', '57']`, losing precisely the identity that matters for contracts.
Hybrid search combines the two not through scores but through ranks: reciprocal
rank fusion with `1/(60 + rank)`. That makes the incomparable scales irrelevant,
and what wins is what both methods agree on — for contracts, the right default.
