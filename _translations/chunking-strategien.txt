title: "Chunking Strategies"
---

The chunk is the unit everything in a RAG system revolves around. It's what gets
embedded, what gets a score, what gets reordered, and what ends up in the prompt.
And it's the decision hardest to take back: cut differently and you rebuild the
index.

This post works through the ways of cutting, the question of the right size, and
two things that regularly get confused — whether chunks may differ in size, and
what the number of vector dimensions has to do with it.

## How large should a chunk be?

There are two limits, and only the upper one is technical.

The technical one: `text-embedding-3-small` accepts at most **8,191 tokens**. Put
in more and you lose the rest — the Azure documentation warns explicitly about
"data loss due to truncation."

The real limit lies far below it, and Microsoft names it in a remarkably honest
sentence:

> Chunking is only required if the source documents are too large for the maximum
> input size imposed by models, but it's also beneficial if content is poorly
> represented as a single vector.

*Poorly represented as a single vector* — that's the point. And to understand it
you have to be clear about what happens during embedding.

![Drei Panels nebeneinander. Links ein zu kleiner Chunk: der Vektor zeigt scharf auf ein einzelnes Thema, aber der Bezug fehlt. In der Mitte ein passender Chunk: ein Thema, vollständig, der Vektor zeigt scharf auf das richtige Thema. Rechts ein zu großer Chunk mit vier Themen: der Vektor ist der Mittelwert der vier Richtungen, kurz, und zeigt in keine davon.](/assets/img/chunking-verduennung.svg)

**The vector always has 1,536 numbers.** Whether ten words go in or eight
hundred. What changes isn't the length of the vector but where it points: it's
averaged over everything in the chunk. A chunk with one topic points sharply at
that topic. A chunk with four topics points at their average — and that lies close
to everything and close to nothing.

Going the other way there's the opposite movement. "Die Frist beträgt drei Monate
zum Monatsende" is a sharp, precise sentence — and worthless as a chunk, because
the referent was in the preceding sentence. Which deadline? Which contract?

Microsoft's starting recommendation is **512 tokens with 25% overlap**. The POC
sits higher at 800, which is defensible for contracts: a paragraph is a unit of
meaning, and you don't like cutting through one.

### "800 tokens" aren't 800 tokens

Except the POC counts differently than the model. `SectionBuilder.py` uses its own
regex:

```python
_TOKEN_RE = re.compile(r"\w+|[^\w\s]", re.UNICODE)
```

The model uses `cl100k_base`. Both are called "tokens" and don't mean the same
thing. Counted on a typical rental contract section:

| | Tokens |
|---|---:|
| POC regex | 165 |
| `cl100k_base` (model) | 313 |
| **Factor** | **1.90** |

The difference comes almost entirely from one property of German:

| Word | POC | Model |
|---|---:|---:|
| `Betriebskostenvorauszahlung` | 1 | 10 |
| `Heizkostenvorauszahlung` | 1 | 9 |
| `Schönheitsreparaturen` | 1 | 8 |
| `1.250,00` | 5 | 5 |

The POC counts a compound as one token, the model breaks it into ten. So a chunk
with `TARGET = 800` is around **1,520 tokens** to the model, and `MAX = 1000` is
around 1,900.

Here that's inconsequential — up to the 8,191 limit there's a factor of 4.3 of
headroom. It would stop being inconsequential if someone set `TARGET = 6000`
assuming that's "safely under 8,191": it would actually be around 11,400, and the
remainder would vanish silently.

## Four ways to cut

![Vier Spalten zeigen denselben Mietvertrag mit sechs Paragraphen, viermal unterschiedlich geschnitten: nach fester Tokenzahl mit gleich großen Chunks und Schnitten mitten im Satz, am Paragraphen mit sehr ungleich großen Chunks, semantisch am Themenwechsel, und hierarchisch mit kleinen Kind-Chunks für die Suche und großen Eltern-Chunks für die Antwort.](/assets/img/chunking-vier-schnitte.svg)

**Fixed token count.** Count to n, cut, continue. Predictable, uniform,
structure-blind — the cut falls wherever the counter stands, mid-sentence if need
be. In Azure AI Search this is the built-in variant: the
[Text Split skill](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/vector-search-how-to-chunk-documents)
with `textSplitMode: pages`, controlled via `maximumPageLength` and
`pageOverlapLength`. Microsoft's default suggestion there: 2,000 characters, 500
characters of overlap. Characters, not tokens — the docs themselves point out the
two don't coincide.

**Cutting at the structure.** With contracts it's natural to cut at the `§`: a
paragraph is a self-contained provision, the boundary is already marked in the
document, and the author set it. The catch is the spread. § 1 (Mietsache) has
forty tokens, § 3 (Miete und Nebenkosten) nineteen hundred. Pure §-cutting
produces an index where chunks differ by a factor of fifty — more on that below.

**Semantic.** Cut at the meaning rather than the form: embed sentences
individually, compare consecutive sentences, and split where similarity drops.
That finds topic changes even within a paragraph. The price shows up at index
build time: one embedding call per sentence instead of per chunk.

**Hierarchical.** Two levels: small chunks for the search, large ones for the
answer. Retrieval goes through the precise child chunk, the parent chunk with the
context goes into the prompt. That resolves the tension between "sharp vector" and
"enough context" — not through a compromise but through two different objects.
Azure AI Search has nothing built in for this; you model it with a field pointing
at the parent section.

### The custom strategy: none of the four, but two of them

The POC does something that fits none of the four boxes, and that's the
interesting part. It counts token-wise up to 800 — and then looks for a cut point
that fits the structure, in this order:

1. a **§ heading** within the next 150 tokens (`GRACE`)
2. otherwise a **line start** within the next 80 (`LINE_SNAP`)
3. otherwise a **sentence boundary** within the next 80 (`SENT_SNAP`)
4. otherwise the same going backwards
5. and finally, hard: never shorter than `MIN = 600`, never longer than
   `MAX = 1000`

The counting is fixed, the boundary is structural, the spread is capped. That's
the sensible combination — and the last line matters more than it looks.

On top of that comes a small thing with a large effect: a variable called `carry`
that takes the most recently seen § heading along into the next chunk. A chunk
from the middle of § 3 therefore still knows it came from § 3. Exactly the context
problem from the left panel above, solved in two lines of code.

## Overlap

![Oben ohne Overlap: ein Satz wird von der Chunkgrenze zerschnitten, Chunk A endet mit „Die Kündigungsfrist beträgt", Chunk B beginnt mit „drei Monate zum Monatsende". Keiner der beiden beantwortet die Frage. Unten mit 100 Token Overlap beginnt Chunk B mit dem Ende von Chunk A und enthält den ganzen Satz. Rechts der Preis: der Index wächst um 12,5 Prozent im POC und um 25 Prozent bei der Microsoft-Empfehlung.](/assets/img/chunking-overlap.svg)

Overlap is the insurance against the unlucky cut. Without it, a sentence can fall
apart such that neither half answers the question — and worse, such that both
vectors point away from the topic. With overlap, every passage appears at least
once in full in the index.

You pay in index size: the POC repeats 100 of 800 tokens, which is 12.5% more
chunks; Microsoft's 128 of 512 is 25%. On top of that comes an effect you see in
the hit list: the same sentence can sit in two chunks and be found twice. The
POC's `per_doc_cap` exists precisely against that.

One warning from the Azure docs is worth quoting: *"setting an overlap value
that's too large can result in no overlap appearing at all"* — choose an overlap
larger than half the chunk size and you get not more safety but none.

## May chunks differ in size?

This is the question where a misconception persists stubbornly. The short answer:
**mathematically yes, without restriction.**

An embedding model produces a fixed-length vector from text of any length — the
variable number of tokens is pooled into a fixed number of dimensions. Ten words
give 1,536 numbers, eight hundred words give 1,536 numbers. Cosine similarity
additionally normalizes for vector length, so the magnitude carries no information
at all. There is no arithmetic operation in vector search that fails on differing
chunk sizes.

**In substance it still isn't irrelevant** — but for the reason given above, not a
mathematical one. The more a chunk contains, the more its vector is an average,
and the closer it moves to the middle of the space. A very long and a very short
chunk are not on equal footing in the same index: the long one is washed out, the
short one sharp. If both fit the question, the short one systematically wins — not
because it fits better, but because it contains less.

From which follows the actual rule, and it's more precise than "the same size":

> Differing sizes are unproblematic. **Unbounded** differences in size are not.

`MIN = 600` and `MAX = 1000` in the POC are therefore not a formality but exactly
that cap: a factor of 1.7 between the smallest and the largest chunk. Pure
§-cutting would reach a factor of fifty. That's why the structural variant alone
rarely suffices.

### And do short questions still map onto long chunks?

The question has eight tokens, the chunk eight hundred. Both go through the same
model into the same space — that works, the models are trained for it. But the
asymmetry leaves a trace: similarity between a short question and a long text
tends to come out lower than between two texts of equal length, precisely because
the long vector is averaged.

For ranking that's initially inconsequential. The search compares candidates
against each other, and an effect that hits all candidates equally doesn't change
the order. It only becomes a problem when it **doesn't** hit them equally — which
is, again, exactly when chunk sizes are far apart.

Incidentally that's an argument for the hybrid search from the
[retrieval post](/rag-auf-vertraegen-in-azure/2026/09/12/retrieval-varianten.html): BM25 has an
explicit length normalization built in with `b = 0.75`. The vector side has
nothing comparable. Combine the two and you get the length correction for free on
at least one of them.

## Are more dimensions better?

![Gruppiertes Balkendiagramm mit drei Embedding-Modellen und zwei Benchmarks. MTEB englisch: ada-002 61,0, 3-small 62,3, 3-large 64,6. MIRACL mehrsprachig: ada-002 31,4, 3-small 44,0, 3-large 54,9. Der Abstand zwischen den Modellen ist auf dem mehrsprachigen Benchmark um ein Vielfaches größer als auf dem englischen. Darunter Dimensionen und Speicherbedarf je 10 000 Chunks.](/assets/img/chunking-dimensionen.svg)

`text-embedding-3-small` delivers 1,536 dimensions, `3-large` delivers 3,072. The
published numbers show two very different pictures, depending on which benchmark
you look at.

On **MTEB** (English) the three models lie 1.3 and 2.3 points apart. That's the
difference for which double the storage hardly pays.

On **MIRACL** (multilingual) it's 12.6 and 10.9 points. That's a different order
of magnitude — and it's the column that counts for German contracts.

On top of that, dimension count and model quality aren't the same thing at all.
Both 3-series models can be shortened via the `dimensions` parameter, and
according to OpenAI a `3-large` shortened to **256 dimensions** still beats an
unshortened `ada-002` at 1,536. Dimensions are a dial for storage and latency, not
the carrier of quality.

### What more dimensions don't solve for contracts

One thing doesn't change with any dimension count. `GUT57` is broken by the
tokenizer into `['G', 'UT', '57']`, and that happens at 3,072 dimensions just as
at 1,536 — it's a property of the tokenizer, not of the vector space. The property
code that carries identity in rental contracts isn't found any better by a larger
embedding model.

For that case the lever isn't the dimension count but BM25 on the other side of
the hybrid search. If you want to upgrade for contracts, upgrade sensibly for
MIRACL — for the multilinguality, not for the vector width.

## What I take from this

**Chunk size isn't a tuning parameter but a decision about what constitutes a unit
of answer.** For contracts that's the paragraph, and a paragraph is sometimes
forty and sometimes nineteen hundred tokens long. Which is exactly why neither
pure token counting nor pure §-cutting is the answer, but the combination: cut at
the structure, cap the spread, carry the heading along.

---

**In short:** A chunk should contain one topic completely and no more — too large
dilutes the vector into an average, too small strips it of its referent. Microsoft
recommends 512 tokens with 25% overlap as a starting point; the POC sits at 800
with 12.5%, where its 800 tokens are around 1,520 to the model, because German
compounds are split differently. Chunks of differing size are mathematically
entirely unproblematic — the vector is always the same length — but unbounded
differences distort the ranking, which is why `MIN`/`MAX` are more than a
formality. And more dimensions aren't better per se: for German contracts the
multilingual benchmark counts, not the vector width, and the property code `GUT57`
is found by BM25 anyway, not by the embedding.
