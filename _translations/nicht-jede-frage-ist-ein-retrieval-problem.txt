title: "Not Every Question Is a Retrieval Problem"
---

"What does § 5 of contract GUT57 say?" — RAG answers that well. "Which unit has
the highest rent?" — RAG does not answer that, and not because the search is bad,
but because it isn't a search question.

This post traces the boundary: which questions are retrieval problems, which
aren't, how to tell the difference — and what to do with the others.

## What retrieval is built for

Top-k is a bet. It reads: *the answer sits in a few places in the corpus, and the
search finds those places.* Ask for the termination clause of a specific contract
and you win the bet — there is exactly one such chunk, it carries the property
code, BM25 finds it, the reranker lifts it to the top. One chunk into the prompt,
done.

Research calls this question **local**: it targets one location. And for local
questions the whole pipeline from the previous posts — chunking, hybrid search,
reranking — is the right answer.

## Where the bet is lost

![Zwei Raster aus zwölf Verträgen mal sechs Paragraphen. Links die lokale Frage nach § 5 von GUT57: genau eine Zelle leuchtet, Top-k findet sie. Rechts die globale Frage nach der höchsten Miete: in allen zwölf Verträgen leuchtet § 3, alle mit ähnlichem Score. Top-3 greift drei davon heraus — eine Stichprobe, keine Antwort.](/assets/img/offene-fragen-lokal-global.svg)

Rental contracts are not a heterogeneous body of knowledge where four chunks fit a
question and a thousand don't. They are **homogeneous**: every contract has a rent
paragraph, every one a termination clause, every one a deposit. Ask about "Miete"
and you get twelve hits with twelve similar scores — and top-3 pulls three of them
out, sorted by wording, not by amount.

That isn't a failure of the search. The search found what it was supposed to find.
The answer just isn't in any of the twelve chunks. It only arises from comparing
all twelve.

Microsoft's GraphRAG paper (Edge et al., 2024) describes exactly this case: *"RAG
fails on global questions directed at an entire text corpus."* The authors call it
query-focused summarization — a question that doesn't need retrieving but
summarizing. With contracts the problem appears in three forms, and they differ in
difficulty.

**Comparison and aggregation.** "Highest rent," "average term," "how many
contracts with a graduated rent." Needs the whole population. On top comes a
technical twist: the number 1,250 isn't searchable by BM25 at all — numeric fields
are excluded from full text in Azure AI Search — and an embedding can't order
numbers. "Greater than" does not exist in vector space.

**Calculation.** "Whose rent can I index in the next two weeks?" The date that *is*
the answer appears in no contract. It follows from lease start, indexation
interval and today's date. Retrieval can find a passage of text. It cannot find a
passage that doesn't exist.

**Absence.** "Which contracts have *no* indexation clause?" You cannot retrieve the
absence of a paragraph. There is no chunk stating that something isn't in there.
That's the hardest of the three forms, and with contracts it's legally relevant: if
no graduated rent is agreed, none applies — a solid statement that no top-k can
deliver.

## The options

### Structure first, then ask

![Oben die Ingestion: ein Vertrag geht in eine Extraktion und wird zu einer typisierten Tabellenzeile mit Objekt, Einheit, Miete als Zahl, Indexierungsklausel als Ja/Nein und nächstem Indexierungsdatum. Unten die Abfrage: „Welche Einheit hat die höchste Miete?" wird zu orderby rent desc top 1, „Wer kann in zwei Wochen indexiert werden?" zu einem Datumsfilter. Kein Retrieval, kein Score.](/assets/img/offene-fragen-strukturieren.svg)

For homogeneous corpora this is the state of the art, and the idea is so simple
it's easy to underestimate: **the model's work moves from question time to index
build time.** Once per contract, a model reads the text and fills a schema — rent
as a number, lease start as a date, indexation clause as yes/no, next indexation
date computed. After that, "highest rent" is no longer a search question but a
query:

```
search=*
$orderby=meta/rent desc
$top=1
```

No retrieval, no score, no model. And the absence question that retrieval
structurally cannot answer becomes trivial:
`$filter=meta/index_clause eq false`.

Azure brings both halves. For the extraction there is, besides a hand-written LLM
prompt, now
[Content Understanding](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/content-understanding/document/overview),
which addresses exactly this case — the docs cite extracting *"contractual
parties, renewal dates, and payment terms in legal agreements"* as an example, with
typed fields that are normalized automatically, and with confidence plus source
grounding per field so uncertain values go to a human. For the query side, Azure AI
Search has
[`$filter` and `$orderby`](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/search/search-filters)
on fields marked `filterable`/`sortable`, and since the 2026-08-01 preview also
facet aggregations: `sum`, `avg`, `min`, `max`, `cardinality` directly in the
search service. Whether you keep the numbers there or in a real database is then a
question of volume, not of principle.

The price: **the schema must have anticipated the question.** Extract "rent" but
not "service charge advance" and you're without an answer again on the next
question. And once per contract means: if the schema changes, the extraction runs
across the entire corpus again.

### Map-reduce across all contracts

For questions the schema doesn't know, the expensive route remains: fetch the
relevant chunk for every contract, have the model extract the value, and end with
one call that compares the n results. That is essentially what GraphRAG does with
its community summaries, just without the graph — with a homogeneous corpus the
contract itself is already the unit you iterate over.

It works, it's complete, it needs no schema. And it costs **n + 1 model calls per
question**. With fourteen contracts that's a rounding error. With fourteen hundred
it's a reason to put the question into the schema after all.

### Routing: the question picks the path

![Eine Frage geht in einen Router. Drei Wege: Retrieval für lokale Fragen mit einem Modellaufruf pro Frage; Strukturabfrage für vorhergesehene globale Fragen mit null bis einem Aufruf; Map-Reduce über alle Verträge für unvorhergesehene globale Fragen mit n plus eins Aufrufen.](/assets/img/offene-fragen-drei-wege.svg)

That gives three paths, and the real architectural question is who decides which
one is taken. Today the answer is almost always: **the model itself, via tools.** A
tool `suche_vertragstext`, a tool `frage_tabelle`, and the model chooses. A local
question lands in retrieval, a global one in the structured query.

Anyone who read the
[text2SQL series](/text2sql-agent-aws/) will recognize this:
once the contracts are structured, "which unit has the highest rent?" *is* a
text2SQL question. The two architectures aren't alternatives but two halves of the
same system — retrieval for the text, SQL for the numbers inside it.

## How I tried it

The POC goes part of this way, and it's worth saying precisely which part.

At index build time, GPT-4.1-nano reads the contract header — the first two
sections, capped at 8,000 characters — with `temperature = 0` and a system prompt
that prescribes normalizations and demands `null` when in doubt. The result lands
as `meta` in the search index: property code, unit codes, street, house number,
postal code, city, tenant, lease start, end of the fixed term. The fields are
declared `filterable` and partly `sortable`.

At query time, retrieval runs first, then `filter_chunks_iterative_exact`: a
substring match of the question against the meta fields, field by field, narrowing
the candidates to the contract the question names. If `GUT57` appears in the
question, only GUT57 chunks remain.

That's an instance of "structure first, then ask" — but for **identity**, not for
**content**. It answers the question "which contract is meant?", and that's exactly
where local questions otherwise fail on the property code. For that it works well.

It does not answer the two questions from the opening, and the reasons are
instructive:

- **The schema doesn't know the rent.** It contains what you need to *find* a
  contract — not what you need to *compare* contracts. No amount, no indexation
  clause, no interval.
- **The filter runs after retrieval.** It narrows the 22 or 60 candidates the
  search delivered. A contract that didn't make it into the candidates cannot be
  filtered in. The index attributes `filterable` and `sortable` are set, but
  `$filter` and `$orderby` are never called — the structure is there, it just isn't
  used as structure.
- **The data are strings.** `start_date` and `fixed_term_end` are of type `String`,
  not `DateTimeOffset`. You can't filter a date range on that, and you can't
  compute a deadline.

The way forward from here is shorter than it sounds: extend the schema with typed
fields (`rent: Double`, `index_clause: Boolean`, `next_index: DateTimeOffset`), run
the extraction across the whole contract instead of only the header, and pose the
question as a structured query *before* retrieval when it is one. The router is
still missing entirely — in the POC every question takes the same path.

## What I take from this

**The question of whether a question is a retrieval problem has to be asked before
retrieval.** Don't ask it, and for global questions you get a sample that looks
like an answer — three contracts with rent figures, neatly cited, and the most
expensive one missing. That's the more dangerous failure than no answer at all.

---

**In short:** Retrieval answers local questions — the answer sits in one place,
top-k finds it. On homogeneous corpora like rental contracts, three question types
fail structurally: comparison and aggregation (needs every contract, and numbers
are neither searchable nor orderable), calculation (the answer appears in no text)
and absence (you can't find what's missing). The state of the art is to have the
model fill a typed schema at index build time and then pose global questions as
`$filter`/`$orderby` or SQL — zero model calls instead of n + 1. A router, usually
the model itself via tools, picks the path. The POC already structures, but only
the identity, only from the contract header, and it uses the structure after
retrieval rather than before.
