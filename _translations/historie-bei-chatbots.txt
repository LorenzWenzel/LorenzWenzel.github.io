title: "How Much History Does a Chatbot Need?"
---

A language model is **completely stateless**. It remembers nothing, it doesn't
know the user, it has no idea what was said ten seconds ago. Whatever it should
know has to be sent along again with every single call.

That isn't a limitation of the current model generation, it's how they work: a
request goes in, an answer comes out, and afterwards the matter is forgotten. The
impression of a conversation arises purely because someone prepends the
transcript so far, every single time.

![Säulendiagramm über sechs Turns: der blaue Anteil bereits früher gesendeter Token wächst stetig, der orange Anteil neuer Token bleibt konstant klein.](/assets/img/historie-zustandslos.svg)

This has an unpleasant consequence: the question from turn 1 is transmitted for
the sixth time in turn 6, and billed for the sixth time. The orange stripe is
everything that's actually new.

Frameworks take this off your hands — LangChain, Strands, AgentCore, every chat
SDK keeps the transcript itself and appends it. That's convenient and exactly
therefore dangerous: **every one of these frameworks brings a strategy with it,
and the default is almost always "keep everything until it no longer fits."**
Decide nothing and you've still decided — just not yourself. It's worth checking
your framework's documentation for what it does on overflow: truncate, summarize,
or crash.

## More history isn't better

The obvious assumption is: the more context, the better the answer. It's wrong,
for two independent reasons.

**The first is length itself.** The research on *context rot* shows that answer
quality degrades as input length grows — markedly so within the advertised
context window, and regardless of whether the additional text is relevant
([Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). On top of that comes
the familiar *lost-in-the-middle* effect: information at the beginning and end is
found more reliably than information in the middle. The mechanism is mundane —
attention is distributed across all tokens, and the more there are, the less
falls on each one.

**The second reason is more uncomfortable.** In *LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn
Conversation* ([arXiv 2505.06120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120), ICLR 2026),
performance across six task types drops by an average of **39%** in multi-turn
conversation compared to the same problem posed in one go. Models scoring above
90% in single-turn land at around 65%.

The decomposition is remarkable: the capability itself drops only 15%, while
**unreliability rises by 112%**. The authors describe the mechanism this way:
models make assumptions early, then over-rely on their own previous answers — and
once they've taken a wrong turn, they don't find their way back.

For the question in this post, that's the decisive sentence. **The history isn't
just expensive, it can also be wrong.** An agent that picked the wrong table in
turn 2 reads that decision as established fact in turn 5. Keep the transcript in
full and you preserve every error along with it.

## How many past turns?

There's no universally valid number, but there is a useful question: **what could
the next user question realistically refer back to?**

For a text2SQL chatbot the answer is reassuringly narrow. The typical follow-ups
are "and now the same for Q3," "only EU-Central please," "sort that by revenue."
They almost always refer to the last turn, rarely to the one before. A question
referring back to turn 1 of a twenty-turn session is the exception — and you
don't build a window for the exception, you build retrieval.

Practice supports this. On a benchmark of 50 tasks, the task completion rate rose
from **71% to 79%** when the history was trimmed to the last five tool pairs;
with an additional running summary, to **91.6%**.

![Balkendiagramm: alles behalten 71 Prozent, letzte fünf Tool-Paare 79 Prozent, zusätzlich laufende Zusammenfassung 91,6 Prozent.](/assets/img/historie-aufraeumen.svg)

The variant that kept everything was the **worst** — at roughly triple the token
consumption ([analysis](https://usewire.io/blog/pruning-agent-context-raised-accuracy/)).
So cleaning up here isn't a cost-saving measure with a quality penalty, it's both
at once.

One caveat belongs here, and it matters: the effect is **not linear in model
strength**. A study across models from 4B to 284B parameters finds an inverted
U-curve — near zero with a weak retriever, up to **11.7 points** of gain when a
good retriever meets a mid-strength model, and **negative** once the model is
strong enough to filter its own context. A model with 80.7% baseline accuracy
lost 1.1 points from masking
([analysis](https://usewire.io/blog/context-pruning-helps-agents-until-it-doesnt/)).

Translated: if you run Claude Sonnet 5 with short sessions, aggressive trimming
gains you little to nothing. If you have a smaller model or long sessions, it
gains you a lot.

## Window, retrieval, or a mixture?

The three building blocks do different things, and you don't choose between them,
you combine them.

![Fünf Zeilen mit je zehn Turn-Blöcken: alles behalten, gleitendes Fenster, Fenster mit Zusammenfassung, Retrieval und eine Mischung aus allem.](/assets/img/historie-strategien.svg)

**The sliding window** is cheap, predictable, and loses abruptly. What falls out
is gone — no warning, no error message. For follow-up questions about the last
turn it's exactly right.

**The running summary** holds the thread beyond the window, and in exchange loses
detail gradually. The difference from a window isn't one of degree but of kind: a
window loses suddenly and completely, a summary slowly and partially. In the
failure case the second is usually more benign — the agent then has a vague
recollection instead of none at all.

**Retrieval** solves a different problem than the other two. In the 2026
literature the split has settled: *long context solves capacity, memory solves
continuity across sessions.* A window can't know what the user asked last week;
a retrieval index can. The price difference is substantial — on the LoCoMo
benchmark, around **6,956 tokens per retrieval call against roughly 26,000 for
full context**
([overview](https://mem0.ai/blog/ai-memory-benchmarks-in-2026)).

Important for expectations: in these studies, full context remains the **upper
bound**, not a bar you clear effortlessly. On LoCoMo it sits at around 87.5%
accuracy, and the good memory systems approach it rather than surpass it.
Switching to retrieval usually trades a few percentage points for an order of
magnitude in tokens and latency. That's a good trade — but it is one.

**The mixture** is the production case: the last three to five turns in full, a
running summary before that, and an index for everything older that the agent
queries when needed.

## Do tool results belong in the history?

This question is the most important of the three, and the least often asked. The
reason sits in a single number: **observation tokens — that is, what tools return
— make up around 84% of an average agent turn.**

Compared to that, questions and answers are rounding errors. Anyone trying to
keep the transcript small while debating the number of turns is optimizing around
in the remaining 16%.

For a text2SQL agent there's an additional asymmetry that nearly makes the
decision by itself:

| Component | Size | Value after the answer |
|---|---|---|
| The user question | tiny | high — follow-ups refer to it |
| The generated SQL | small | **high** — shows which tables and filters applied |
| The result set | large | **near zero** — the answer is long since formulated |
| The formulated answer | small | high |

The SQL *is* the summary of the result set that you'd otherwise have to generate.
In twenty lines it tells the model what was just discussed — which table, which
period, which filters. The 1,200 tokens of result rows tell it the same thing, at
sixty times the price.

The practical rule is therefore: **keep the SQL, replace the rows.** Instead of
the full result set, a placeholder stays in the history:

```
[Ergebnis verworfen: 1 248 Zeilen, 6 Spalten, Summe net_revenue_eur 4,21 Mio. €]
```

That's entirely sufficient for "and now the same for Q3." For the rare case where
the user does refer to a specific row, the agent can re-run the query — an extra
model call, but only then.

The common approach is tiered: watch token utilization at 70%, replace old tool
results with placeholders from 85%, and only at 95% have the model produce a real
summary. For Claude models there's context editing with
`clear_tool_uses_20250919`, which removes old results instead of summarizing
them.

## The conflict with the cache

One point missing from almost every guide: **every cleanup invalidates the prompt
cache from the cut point onward.**

The cache is a prefix comparison. Replace a tool result in the middle of the
history with a placeholder and you've changed everything from that point on — and
on the next call you pay full price for the entire remainder. How expensive that
is, is covered in
[Why Prompt Caching Is Mandatory for text2SQL Agents](/text2sql-agent-aws/2026/06/28/prompt-caching-pflicht.html).

From which follows a rule that contradicts intuition: **clean up rarely and in
large steps, not continuously and in small ones.** Trim a little on every turn and
you permanently have no cache. Clean up hard once every ten turns and you pay for
the rebuild once and profit in between.

## A proposal for the text2SQL case

As a starting point, not as truth:

- **The last 3 turns** in full, including tool results.
- **Older turns**: keep question, SQL and answer; replace result sets with a
  placeholder carrying row count and a key figure.
- **From around 15 turns**, a running summary that records the subject of the
  conversation — which tables, which period, which definitions were clarified.
- **Across sessions**, only what the user explicitly saved or what holds
  permanently as domain fact. Not yesterday's conversation.
- **Clean up in blocks**, not on every turn.

And a warning that follows from the multi-turn finding: when the agent has
visibly gone off the rails, the best intervention isn't to ask again but to
**start over** — with the original question, posed more precisely. According to
the study, a model that has taken a wrong turn rarely recovers within the same
conversation. A "new question" button in the chatbot is therefore not an
interface detail but a quality measure.


---

**In short:** The model remembers nothing; the history is something you actively
send along and pay for again on every turn. More of it isn't better: quality
declines with length, and in multi-turn conversation it drops by an average of
39% — mainly because models cling to their own earlier mistakes. The most
important lever isn't the turns but the tool results: they make up around 84% of
the transcript and are nearly worthless once the answer is written. For text2SQL
that means: keep the SQL, replace the rows with a placeholder, last three turns
in full, condense what's older — and do it in large steps rather than
continuously, or the cache picks up the tab.
